Six passages

Chapter 2: The Loop

The thought arrives at 6:40 while I am doing nothing that invites it. That is the first thing you must understand: it is not summoned. I am filling a steel tumbler with water, the tap is running, somewhere a temple loudspeaker is warming up for the evening, and the thought is simply there, inserted between one half-second and the next, with the seamlessness of a cut in a film.

…

And on the heels of it, arriving like its shadow, the rule. The rule is always in second person. If you don't put the tumbler down and fill it again, properly this time, with the right mind, it will happen.

I know the rule is absurd.

…

Knowing changes nothing. Knowing stands off to one side with its arms folded while the hand, my hand, pours the water out and begins again. Properly. With the right mind.

…

It came, on average, forty times a day, for years.

Chapter 4: The Machine That Sees Sideways

In 2017 I began teaching machines to see. I want to show you what that work actually is, step by step, because one idea from it changed my life. Here is the idea, before anything else: a machine that learns can only know the world we feed it. It has no way to see past its own inputs. And I could not find one good reason why that rule should stop at machines.

…

At every junction sits a little gate that decides how strongly to pass the signal on.

What the gate does is plain: weigh the evidence, add your bias, decide what gets through.

…

In my trade, bias does not mean prejudice. It means a setting. It is where the gate sits before any evidence turns up, how much persuading this particular junction wants before it lets the signal through.

…

Every part of that machine has one, because there is no way to make a decision without one. Deciding just is holding a threshold. Which means the useful question was never whether you carry biases. The question is who set yours, and when.

Chapter 9: The Wallpaper Universe

In a video game, the world behind you does not exist.

I mean that literally, not poetically. Stand in a game landscape, a forest, a city, a battlefield, and the mountain at your back is not sitting there quietly, waiting for you. It is not hidden behind you. It has not been built.

Building it has a name. We call it rendering: working out, dot by dot, what colour every point on your screen should be. Rendering costs the machine real effort, and the machine has only so much effort to give, because it has to finish an entire new picture around sixty times every second. So it spends that effort only on what you are looking at right now. Everything outside your view is kept as instructions rather than as scenery. A recipe, not a cake.

Then you turn your head. In the few thousandths of a second before your eyes settle, the machine builds the mountain, rock, haze, birds. When you turn away again, it lets the mountain go back to being a recipe.

No player has ever caught a world half-built. Not because the trick is subtle. Because of the order the two events happen in. The turning of your head is the very thing that triggers the building. The seam cannot be caught, by anyone, ever, because looking is what closes it.

We did not invent this idea. We reinvented it, and we were three hundred years late.

Chapter 10: The Body Clock

You have never experienced time.

You think you have. Go looking for the experience itself, though, and it comes apart in your hands. What you actually experience is change. Hunger arriving. Light moving along a wall. A song ending. What you actually keep is memory: a stack of those changes, filed in order.

"Time" is the name we gave to the filing.

…

Which means a clock is not a window onto time. A clock is a very boring body whose ageing we have all agreed to trust.

…

Why does time speed up as you get older? Every adult feels it. My notes worked the mechanism out on a bus, and it is not mysterious once you see it.

Chapter 11: The One-Boxer

Here is where I stand on free will, stated up front so you can watch me defend it: you are free at breakfast, and not at the scale of your biography.

I mean that almost exactly.

This morning you chose, genuinely, I am not going to take it from you, between the dosa and the bread. The choosing was real at the scale where it happened. Preferences consulted. A small verdict issued. Jam.

What I no longer believe, after the life this book has laid out, is that the large line was ever as open. Whether you would be born to that kitchen. Whether you would meet that spouse, carry that illness, lose that faith.

…

The river is scheduled. The eddies are yours.

Chapter 12: Older Than Bostrom

That is the ancestry. A suspicion filed independently for something like three thousand years, from Ujjain to Athens to Copenhagen.

Impressive, no?

No, says the prosecution, rising. And I will tell you why.

Let me introduce him properly.

He was born the same year as my OCD, in the same building. A boy who checked and doubted and audited everything, including, eventually, mercifully, the disease itself. He is the one who told you the rituals were absurd even while my hands were performing them. He is not my enemy. He is the sanest tenant I have.

…

"And what," the skeptic asks, leaning in, "has this entire book been? A man who spends his days inside neural networks looked up at his life, his dead and his universe, and found neural networks."
